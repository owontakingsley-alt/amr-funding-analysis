Key insight
"AMR is the only major infectious disease where funding has fallen as deaths have risen."

The G7 and WHO have identified AMR as a top-3 global health threat — but funding commitments remain fractional compared to diseases with comparable or lower death tolls. No new antibiotic class has reached market since 1984.

HIV/AIDS — investment drove the decline
−76%

HIV deaths fell from a peak of ~2.1M (2004) to ~630K by 2023. PEPFAR and UNAIDS drove $18.56B in annual investment by 2019 — $26,896 per death. The causal link between funding and lives saved is direct.

Malaria — sustained response, real results
−34%

WHO WMR 2024 shows malaria deaths fell from 860,796 (2000) to 566,918 (2019) — a 34% reduction driven by the Global Fund and PMI/USAID. COVID-19 pushed deaths back up to 621,900 in 2020, recovering to 596,781 by 2023.

AMR — barely moved in 30 years
+16%

AMR attributable deaths grew from ~1.1M (2000) to 1.27M (2019) — while HIV was falling 76%. No PEPFAR equivalent. No global AMR Fund. No subscription mechanism. No international response mechanism of comparable scale. The Lancet 2024 forecasts 1.91M AMR deaths per year by 2050 on current trajectory. The trajectory will not change without structural intervention.

Equity gap analysis
If AMR received the same funding-per-death as Tuberculosis ($4,143 — the next-lowest), total AMR research spending would need to increase by 226%.

Matching HIV's per-death funding would require a 2,014% increase in annual AMR R&D. Even reaching the median of the other diseases (~$8,700/death) would require a 584% increase.

The gap isn't about awareness — it's about structural market failure. No commercial incentive exists to develop new antibiotics when existing ones are priced as commodities. Subscription models and advance purchase commitments are the only proven solutions.
